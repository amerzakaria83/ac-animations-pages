    Measure your tinnitus with headphones

    This follows the measurements an audiologist uses to describe tinnitus: its pitch, how loud it is, how much sound covers it, and whether it fades once that sound stops. Plan on 15 to 25 minutes in a quiet room.

    Before you start

    • Noise cancelling on, transparency off. ANC blocks room noise. Turn off ambient, transparency and conversation modes.
    • Turn off sound processing. Disable spatial audio, EQ presets, adaptive or personalised sound, and any volume limiter that changes levels on its own.
    • Wired is best. Bluetooth works, but some connections drop sounds above about 15 kHz.
    • Set your computer volume to about half, then leave it alone. Every level here is measured against that setting.
    • Expect your tinnitus to seem louder. Silence and noise cancelling make it stand out, which helps matching. Some ANC headphones add a faint hiss of their own. Listen with nothing playing for a few seconds so you know what that sounds like.
    Press Esc or Stop sound at the top at any time. Levels are capped, but your volume setting decides how loud the cap is. If anything is uncomfortable, stop and turn it down.

    Check left and right

    Play a beep in each ear. If they come out on the wrong side, swap your headphones or check the balance setting on your computer.

    Set a comfortable level

    Play the 1 kHz reference tone and adjust it until it is clear and comfortable, about as loud as a quiet conversation. Never uncomfortable. This becomes the starting point and the soft ceiling for every other step.

    Hearing check

    Finds the quietest beep you can hear at each frequency in each ear. Tinnitus often sits near a frequency where hearing has dipped, so this gives your pitch match some context.

    These levels are in decibels relative to your device's maximum output, not the dB HL of a clinic audiogram. Compare your ears and frequencies with each other. Most headphones also lose sensitivity above 8 kHz, so high-frequency results look worse for everyone.

    You will hear three short beeps after a random pause. Press I hear it (or Space) as soon as you hear them, even if they are very faint. Don't press if you're unsure.

    Play the matching sounds in

    When tinnitus is on one side, clinics often play the matching sound in the other ear so it can't cover the tinnitus up. Start with the same ear. If the test sound seems to blend into your tinnitus, switch to the opposite ear.

    Pitch match

    Find the frequency that sounds most like your tinnitus. Judge the pitch, not the loudness. If one sound is harder to hear, turn the level up a little.

    Listen to A and B, then pick the one closer in pitch to your tinnitus. Each round narrows the range by half.

    Loudness match

    First find the quietest level you can hear at your tinnitus pitch. Then raise the sound until it is as loud as your tinnitus. The difference is your tinnitus loudness in dB SL (decibels above your threshold).

    Do the pitch match first so this step knows which sound to use.

    1. Threshold at

    Three short beeps after a random pause. Press I hear it or Space as soon as you hear them.

    2. Match the loudness

    Play the sound and adjust it in small steps until it is equally loud as your tinnitus. If you can't decide, choose the level just before it got louder.

    Minimum masking level

    How much noise does it take to cover your tinnitus completely? A low number suggests background sound may give you relief.

    1. Noise threshold

    Short bursts of noise after a random pause. Press I hear it or Space when you hear them.

    2. Cover the tinnitus

    Start the noise, then raise it 1 dB at a time until you can no longer hear your tinnitus at all, not just less of it.

    Minimum masking level—dB above your noise threshold

    Residual inhibition

    Play noise slightly above your masking level for a minute, then stop. Many people find their tinnitus goes quiet or disappears for a while afterwards. How long it stays away is useful to know.

      Relief sounds

      Background sound makes tinnitus less noticeable for many people. Keep it a little quieter than your tinnitus so the two blend, rather than drowning it out.

      Gap centred on

      Notched sound therapy has mixed evidence. Treat it as something to try, not a cure. Keep the level low and take breaks.

      Tinnitus Match is a self-check tool, not a medical device. Headphone output isn't calibrated, so levels are relative to your own setup. Repeat it with the same headphones and volume setting when comparing over time. For persistent or bothersome tinnitus, see an audiologist or ENT.
